A B S T R A C T
Accurately predicting the carbon dioxide (CO2) adsorption capacity of magnesium oxide (MgO)-based sorbents is 
crucial for advancing carbon capture technologies. This study develops an interpretable machine learning 
framework to address this challenge. Fourteen candidate models were evaluated, with six top-performing ones 
selected for Bayesian hyperparameter optimization using the Optuna framework. A novel weighted ensemble 
model was then constructed by integrating the entropy weight method and the Technique for Order Preference 
by Similarity to Ideal Solution. The optimized ensemble model demonstrated outstanding performance on an 
independent test set, achieving an R2 of 0.9903 with MSE, RMSE, and MAE of 0.361, 0.6008, and 0.2544 
respectively, surpassing most individual models. SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis identified material 
morphology as the most significant feature (43.8% contribution), particularly granular morphology. Process 
parameters including time (23.3%) and temperature (14.1%) were the next major contributors, while pore 
structure variables collectively contributed 15.3%. The analysis further revealed complex non-linear de­
pendencies and interaction effects, with the combination of medium temperature (200-400 ◦C) and adsorption 
time (100-250 min) proving optimal. Additionally, a web application was deployed to provide real-time pre­
diction capability. This work provides both a reliable predictive tool and valuable mechanistic insights for 
designing high-performance MgO-based CO2 adsorbents.
1. Introduction
In response to the escalating severity of global climate change, car­
bon dioxide (CO2) capture and storage technology represents a key 
pathway toward the attainment of carbon neutrality goals. Among 
various adsorbent materials, metal oxide-based adsorbents have 
received significant attention due to their high theoretical adsorption 
capacity and renewability. In particular, magnesium oxide (MgO) based 
materials exhibit excellent CO2 adsorption potential in the medium- 
temperature range (Gao et al., 2017a). Compared with other adsor­
bents, MgO stands out as a promising candidate owing to its high 
theoretical CO2 adsorption capacity, relatively mild operating condi­
tions, abundant raw material sources, excellent recyclability, and the 
tunability of its surface alkalinity and pore structure via strategies like 
morphology control and doping (Alhalawani et al., 2025; Figueiredo 
et al., 2024; Perera et al., 2024). However, the CO2 adsorption perfor­
mance of this material is governed by a multitude of factors, including 
material morphology (Tan et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2020), pore structure 
(Song et al., 2015), the nature of dopants (Dal Pozzo et al., 2019; Trivi˜no 
et al., 2018), and process conditions (Kwon et al., 2023; Chai et al., 
2024). The existence of complex nonlinear interactions among these 
variables poses a significant challenge for traditional methods that 
depend on experimental trial-and-error or linear regression. This limi­
tation obstructs accurate prediction of adsorption behavior, conse­
quently hampering efficient material design and process optimization.
Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful means of deciphering the 
complex structure-activity relationships inherent to these systems. 
These techniques have proven successful in predicting diverse adsorp­
tion processes, showcasing their capacity to model multivariate and 
nonlinear interactions (Zhang et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 
2024a; Application of machine learning in, 2022; Zhao et al., 2025a). 
Although previous investigations have utilized empirical models or 
standalone machine learning approaches for predicting adsorption 
performance (Yu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024; Liu

multi-variable coupling effects. Especially in the prediction of 
MgO-based adsorbents, existing studies mostly focus on the preliminary 
application of a single model, such as Decision Tree (Lu et al., 2025) and 
Gradient Boosting Regression Trees (GBRT) (Fan et al., 2025). Conse­
quently, an integrated strategy capable of synergistically enhancing 
both predictive accuracy and model stability is notably absent. More­
over, a systematic quantification of the contribution of individual fea­
tures to the adsorption capacity, as well as their complex interactions, 
remains underdeveloped (Nguyen et al., 2024). This gap impedes the 
establishment of a foundational principle for the rational design of 
high-performance adsorbents. Therefore, the development of an inte­
grated ML framework that simultaneously delivers high predictive ac­
curacy and robust interpretability is of paramount importance for 
advancing the rational design and process optimization of MgO-based 
adsorbents.
This study presents an integrated ML framework that combines 
ensemble modeling with interpretability analysis to accurately predict 
the CO2 uptake of MgO-based adsorbents. A systematic comparison of 
14 ML algorithms is conducted, leading to the identification of six top- 
performing models for subsequent hyperparameter optimization. The 
entropy weight method and the Technique for Order Preference by 
Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method are employed to construct 
a weighted ensemble model. This novel approach significantly enhances 
prediction accuracy and robustness. Furthermore, the framework in­
tegrates SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), partial dependence 
plots (PDP), and accumulated local effects (ALE) analyses to systemat­
ically quantify the influence of morphology, pore structure, doping type, 
and process parameters on adsorption performance, along with their 
nonlinear interactions. This interpretable approach reveals the domi­
nant factors governing adsorption capacity, including granular 
morphology, specific intermediate ranges of temperature and time, and 
metal oxide doping.
2. Data and methods
2.1. Data collection
The dataset on CO2 adsorption by MgO-based adsorbents was con­
structed from literature retrieved from the Web of Science core collec­
tion database, covering publications between 2015 and 2025. The 
literature search was conducted using key terms, namely “carbon di­
oxide,” “magnesium oxide,” and “adsorption.” To ensure comprehen­
siveness, an initial set of publications was expanded by examining the 
citation networks of relevant articles identified in the search. Data 
pertaining to CO2 uptake and adsorption process conditions were ac­
quired from the main text or supplementary tables of relevant publica­
tions. When data were exclusively presented graphically, the 
corresponding isotherms, kinetic curves, or performance profiles were 
digitally extracted using the Plot Digitizer software. To ensure accuracy, 
each figure was calibrated against its axis scales prior to digitization. For 
each critical data point, three independent extraction iterations were 
performed, and the mean value was recorded as the final result. The 
standard deviation derived from the triplicate extractions was docu­
mented to quantify the uncertainty associated with the digitization 
process. If the standard deviation for any data point exceeded 3% of its 
mean value, the figure was recalibrated and the extraction process was 
repeated.
A systematic cross-checking procedure was applied to all digitized 
data. The procedure comprised two principal steps: (i) replotting the 
extracted data points against the original figures to verify trend con­
sistency, and (ii) directly comparing the extracted key parameter values 
with any numerically reported values in the source texts. Data points 
exhibiting significant discrepancies during cross-checking were verified 
and corrected when necessary. This verification was conducted by re- 
examining the original publication or consulting relevant literature, 
thereby ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the final compiled 
dataset. Ultimately, data from 19 key studies (Tan et al., 2020; Song 
et al., 2015; Chai et al., 2024; Li et al., 2017; Mn-promoted MgO ad­
sorbents with boosted, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025c; Gong et al., 2023; Ding 
et al., 2025; Gui et al., 2024; Alkadhem et al., 2020; Jin et al., 2020; Niu 
et al., 2021; Alkadhem et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2018; Ding et al., 2015; Gao 
et al., 2017b; Ding et al., 2021; Firmansyah et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024) 
were systematically compiled to construct the final dataset.
2.2. Data processing
An exploratory data analysis (EDA) was performed on the original 
dataset to characterize its initial distribution and to establish a foun­
dation for subsequent preprocessing. The results of this EDA, including 
box plots for all numerical variables, are presented in Fig. S1. In terms of 
structural parameters, the SBET had a median value of approximately 
100 m2/g, with an interquartile range of 50 to 150 m2/g. This suggested 
that most adsorbents possessed a medium to low specific surface area, 
although some samples modified by doping or activation exhibited 
values as high as 500 m2/g. The Vpore distribution showed a median of 
about 0.3 cm3/g, with most values ranging from 0.1 to 0.4 cm3/g; 
however, maximum values approached 1.0 cm3/g. The pores were 
predominantly mesoporous, with a median dpore of approximately 12 
nm and a main range of 5-20 nm; the maximum observed pore size was 
60 nm. Particle sizes were mainly distributed between 10 and 100 nm, 
with isolated outliers observed at approximately 8000 nm. Regarding 
process conditions, the median pressure was close to 0.1 MPa, and the 
data exhibited a very narrow range of 0.05-0.15 MPa. This suggested 
that the dataset predominantly comprised experiments conducted at or 
near ambient pressure, with a limited number of high-pressure experi­
ments reaching a maximum of approximately 1.0 MPa. The median 
temperature was approximately 200 ◦C, and the data main ranged from 
150 to 250 ◦C, with an upper outlier near 500 ◦C. The median adsorption 
time was about 100 min, with values typically between 50 and 150 min 
and a maximum duration of 350 min, which covered the testing re­
quirements of both short-term kinetic studies and long-term equilibrium 
assessments. The CO2 uptake had a median value of approximately 5 
mmol/g, generally ranging from 2 to 8 mmol/g, while some modified 
samples exhibited uptakes as high as 30 mmol/g. The EDA revealed key 
characteristics of the original dataset, including the statistical distribu­
tion, central tendency, and presence of outliers for each variable. 
Furthermore, it confirmed that the dataset encompasses a wide diversity 
of MgO-based adsorbent types and experimental conditions, which es­
tablishes a solid foundation for developing robust machine learning 
models.
A data processing workflow was implemented to build a high-quality 
dataset and ensure the reliability of subsequent machine learning 
models. This pipeline commenced with data cleaning and missing value 
treatment. The missing rate for each feature was quantified and is 
detailed in Table S1. Several features, including dpore, particle size, Vpore, 
and morphology, contained missing values. As the missing rate for each 
of these features was below the predefined 30% exclusion threshold, all 
were retained for analysis. Missing values in the numerical features were 
addressed through an iterative imputation approach (Ribeiro and Frei­
tas, 2021). The impact of imputation was assessed by comparing key 
statistical summaries of the continuous variables, namely the mean, 
standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis, before and after the pro­
cedure; these results are compiled in Table S2. For categorical features, 
missing entries were filled using the most frequent category (mode), 
which represents the most frequent category. The distributions of the 
categorical variables are summarized in Table S3. The imputation pro­
cess preserved the overall data distribution well without introducing 
significant bias, thereby ensuring the reliability of the subsequent 
modeling work. Outlier detection was performed using the Isolation 
Forest algorithm (Puggini and McLoone, 2018). A sensitivity analysis

Different encoding strategies were applied to the categorical vari­
ables to balance model performance, feature dimensionality, and 
interpretability. Specifically, the Morphology variable comprises eight 
unordered categories with no inherent, quantifiable ordinal relationship 
among them. Consequently, one-hot encoding was applied to avoid 
imposing spurious ordinal assumptions. In contrast, the Dopant or 
modifier variable contains 39 distinct categories. Using one-hot encod­
ing for this variable would create high-dimensional sparse features, 
potentially inducing model overfitting and increased computational 
complexity. Therefore, to manage the feature dimensionality, label 
encoding was employed for this variable.
Although label encoding was applied, its potential to introduce 
spurious ordinal relationships was addressed during the model inter­
pretation phase. The specific impact of the dopant or modifier variable 
on adsorption capacity was elucidated using Partial Dependence Plots 
(PDP) and Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) plots, ensuring a robust 
interpretability analysis for this key feature. A comparative evaluation 
revealed that the predictive performance of models capable of natively 
handling categorical features, such as CatBoost, was comparable to the 
performance achieved using a uniform encoding scheme across all 
models. Detailed performance comparisons are provided in Table S4 and 
Fig. S3, with corresponding hyperparameters listed in Table S5. There­
fore, a uniform encoding scheme was adopted. This approach guaran­
teed consistent feature representation for all models involved in 
comparative and ensemble learning analyses, which was essential for 
ensuring fair model comparisons and reliable ensemble weighting.
All numerical features were standardized to eliminate scale differ­

2.3. Model development and optimization
A two-stage machine learning pipeline was implemented for the 
development of high-performance predictive models. In the first stage, a 
preliminary screening of candidate models was conducted. Fourteen 
candidate regression algorithms were trained and evaluated under their 
default hyperparameter configurations. The objective of this stage was a 
rapid comparative assessment rather than the generation of deployable 
models or precise performance metrics. This approach enabled the 
identification of algorithms with fundamental promise and the elimi­
nation of clearly underperforming ones, thereby concentrating compu­
tational resources on the subsequent, more intensive hyperparameter 
optimization stage. Based on their performance in this initial evaluation, 
the six top-performing algorithms were selected to proceed to the second 
stage. This two-stage design facilitated the efficient identification of 
algorithm types well-suited to the specific dataset, while minimizing 
initial computational overhead. The second stage focused on hyper­
parameter optimization and rigorous model evaluation. A Bayesian 
optimization framework was applied to tune the hyperparameters for 
each of the six shortlisted models to maximize their predictive perfor­
mance. The Optuna framework served as the optimization engine, 
employing Sequential Model-Based Optimization (Wang et al., 2023) to

was evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation.

A weighted ensemble strategy was adopted to enhance the robust­
ness and accuracy of the predictive model. This approach mitigated the 
reliance on the performance variance of any single model by leveraging 
the complementary strengths of several high-performing base learners. 
A critical aspect of this ensemble approach involved the objective 
assignment of weights to each base learner. Accordingly, a weighting 
strategy based on multi-criteria decision-making was introduced to 
systematically rank and assign these weights. The entropy weight 
method (Li et al., 2024) was utilized to automatically assign objective 
weights to each evaluation metric according to the dispersion degree of 
its values, thereby eliminating the subjectivity inherent in manual 
weight assignment. Subsequently, the Technique for Order Preference 
by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) method (Ribeiro and Freitas, 
2021) was applied. This process involved calculating the distances from 
each model to both a positive ideal solution (defined by the optimal 
value for each metric) and a negative ideal solution (defined by the 
worst value for each metric). A relative closeness score to the ideal so­
lution was subsequently computed for each model. This score serves as a 
composite rating that encapsulates the overall performance of each 
model across all evaluated dimensions. Compared to the selection of a 
single best model based on a solitary metric or the use of a simple 
average across models, the entropy-weighted TOPSIS approach provides 
a more integrated and nuanced strategy. This method leverages multi­
dimensional performance data and assigns objective, data-driven 
weights to the various metrics, thereby facilitating a more balanced 
and robust model assessment. Based on this methodology, a weighted 
ensemble model was constructed. The integration weight for each base 
learner was set to be proportional to its normalized TOPSIS closeness 
score, ensuring that learners with a higher composite score, indicative of 
superior overall performance, exerted greater influence on the final 
ensemble predictions. Initially, the entropy weight method (Chen et al., 
2025) was employed to automatically assign weights to the evaluation 
metrics according to their dispersion, thereby minimizing subjective 
bias. Subsequently, the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to 
Ideal Solution (Zare Banadkouki, 2023) was applied to calculate the 
relative closeness of each model to the ideal solution, which yielded a 
comprehensive performance ranking. A weighted ensemble model was 
then developed based on this ranking. The integration weight for each 
base learner was assigned proportionally to its TOPSIS closeness, 
granting higher influence in the final prediction to models with superior 
rankings. This ensemble approach leverages the strengths of individual 
models to enhance both the robustness and accuracy of predictions.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Development of ML models
3.1.1. Model selection
Fourteen pre-selected machine learning models were initially 
executed using their default hyperparameters and evaluated based on 
MSE, RMSE, MAE, and R2. Prioritizing test set performance was critical 
for evaluating model generalization, mitigating overfitting, and pre­
venting data leakage, thereby providing a reliable basis for model se­
lection and subsequent optimization. The comprehensive performance 
ranking of all evaluated models on the test set was presented in Fig. S5. 
Based on the TOPSIS ranking results, the six top-performing models 
were selected for the subsequent hyperparameter optimization stage, 
namely CatBoost, Extra Trees, XGBoost, Random Forest, GBRT, and 
LightGBM. Notably, all selected models belonged to the category of 
ensemble learning algorithms fundamentally built upon decision trees. 
Their superior performance was not coincidental but primarily origi­
nated from an inherent compatibility between their algorithmic prop­
erties and the characteristics of the adsorption dataset investigated in 
this study. The relationships between adsorption capacity and process 
parameters are typically complex and nonlinear, involving significant 
interaction effects. Tree-based models exhibit an inherent proficiency in 
capturing such patterns. Specifically, Bagging algorithms such as 
Random Forest and Extra Trees enhance generalization capability by 
reducing model variance. In contrast, Boosting algorithms, for example 
XGBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM, and GBRT, focus on bias reduction 
through sequential and iterative refinement.
3.1.2. Hyperparameter optimization
Due to its heightened sensitivity to large errors and favorable 
interpretability, RMSE was selected as the objective function for the 
optimization process (Hodson, 2022). In each trial, the model was 
trained on the training set, and the RMSE was calculated on the vali­
dation fold. The average RMSE across all five folds subsequently served 
as the evaluation metric for the corresponding hyperparameter set. A 
total of 150 trials were conducted with the Optuna framework to bal­
ance computational efficiency against search comprehensiveness. The 
hyperparameter combination that yielded the lowest cross-validation 
RMSE was independently chosen for each model. A final evaluation 
was then conducted on a separate test set that had been excluded from 
all training and optimization procedures, providing an unbiased esti­
mation of the generalization capability of the models.
The optimal performance metrics of the six models after hyper­
parameter optimization are summarized in Table S6, and the corre­
sponding hyperparameter sets are detailed in Table S7. As shown in 
Fig. 1, the CatBoost model demonstrated exceptional performance after 
hyperparameter tuning, achieving R2 values of 0.9954 on the training 
set and 0.9892 on the test set. These results signify an excellent fitting 
capability and outstanding generalization performance. The model also 
exhibited low MSE (0.3015) and RMSE (0.5491) on the test set, con­
firming its high predictive accuracy and stability. The Extra Trees model 
achieved a perfect fit on the training set (R2 = 1.000), with a test set R2 
of 0.9774. Although this test set performance was slightly lower, it still 
indicates strong predictive stability, which is further substantiated by its 
low error metrics (MSE = 0.3195, RMSE = 0.5652). Both the XGBoost 
and Random Forest models also exhibited strong performance. XGBoost 
attained R2 values of 0.9830 on the training set and 0.9717 on the test 
set. Despite its relatively high test set MSE (0.7937) and RMSE (0.8909), 
the model maintained satisfactory predictive reliability. Similarly, the 
Random Forest model achieved R2 values of 0.9935 and 0.9742 for the 
training and test sets, respectively. Its error metrics (MSE = 0.7970, 
RMSE = 0.8928) were comparable to those of XGBoost; however, 
Random Forest demonstrated superior performance in terms of MAE 
(0.2070). In contrast, the LightGBM and GBRT models exhibited 
comparatively inferior performance. GBRT, in particular, showed

3.1.3. Model integration
The employed evaluation metrics included MSE, RMSE, MAE, and 
R2. These metrics differ not only in their scales but also in their funda­
mental optimization objectives. Specifically, MSE, RMSE, and MAE are 
error-minimization metrics, while R2 is a goodness-of-fit metric to be 
maximized. To facilitate an equitable and consistent multi-criteria 
evaluation, a preprocessing transformation was applied. The error 
metrics, namely MSE, RMSE, and MAE, were first converted into 
maximization-type metrics. This conversion ensured that for all metrics, 
a higher numerical value consistently indicated superior model perfor­
mance. Subsequently, to eliminate the influence of disparate measure­
ment scales, the transformed metric values were normalized to a 
uniform range of zero to one using Min-Max normalization. This two- 
step preprocessing established a consistent and dimensionless basis for 
the subsequent assignment of objective weights in the comprehensive 
evaluation framework. For weight determination, the entropy weight 
method was employed as an objective alternative to subjective weight­
ing approaches. This method calculated weights based on the inherent 
variability in the data of each evaluation indicator, with the computed 
weights detailed. Indicators demonstrating greater variability were 
considered to carry more information and were consequently assigned 
higher weights within the comprehensive evaluation system. This 
methodology ensured mathematical rigor and objectivity in the weight 
assignment process, effectively minimized potential biases introduced 
by subjective human judgment.
The objective weights determined for each evaluation metric via the 
entropy weight method are summarized in Table S8. RMSE received the 
highest weight. This outcome indicated that the performance variation 
across the base learners was most discernible with respect to the RMSE, 
which consequently afforded this metric the greatest discriminative 
power within the integrated assessment. Despite the inherent mathe­
matical relationship between MSE and RMSE, both metrics were allo­
cated considerable weights. This weighting result implied that MSE and 
RMSE provided complementary information from distinct analytical 
perspectives to the overall model evaluation, thereby jointly enabling a 
more comprehensive performance assessment. Following the determi­
nation of indicator weights, the TOPSIS method was employed to 
conduct a comprehensive ranking of the base models. First, the original 
data matrix was weighted and normalized using the previously obtained 
entropy-based indicator weights. Subsequently, the positive ideal solu­
tion and the negative ideal solution were identified. The relative close­
ness of each model to these ideal solutions was then computed. This 
relative closeness, which serves as a comprehensive score index, formed 
the basis for the final ranking. The resulting relative closeness values 
were then normalized to establish the final weighting coefficients for 
each model within the integrated framework, as summarized in Table 1. 
This systematic approach guaranteed that models demonstrating supe­
rior and more consistent performance received higher weights in the 
final ensemble, enabling the integrated model to effectively leverage the 
complementary strengths of all base learners. The resulting predictive

certain specific tasks or datasets, the ensemble approach might not 
outperform the best individual model across every single metric. The 
strength of the ETFM, however, originated from its balanced stability 
and generalization capability. By effectively integrating the advantages 
of multiple base models, the ETFM mitigated the risks of overfitting or 
underfitting that could occur when relying on a single model. This was 
clearly evidenced by comparing the performance of the ETFM with the

3.2. Model interpretation
3.2.1. Independent feature interpretability analysis
After excluding the variable “Dopant or modifier,” which was 
numerically encoded through label encoding and could potentially 
introduce interpretative bias, a global SHAP analysis and feature 
importance assessment were conducted with the ETFM model (Fig. 3). 
The feature importance decomposition indicated that morphological 
information was the most significant contributor, accounting for 43.8% 
of the total influence. Among these morphological features, the Granular 
(23.0%) and Cube (9.6%) morphologies exerted the strongest influence, 
followed by the Flower-like (6.5%) and Flake (3.8%) morphologies. In 
contrast, the Rod-shaped (0.4%), Sphere (0.3%), Nest-like (0.1%), and 
Block morphologies, the latter contributing approximately 0%, exhibi­
ted negligible effects. This importance ranking corresponded closely to 
the average predicted CO2 uptake for each morphological category. The 
Granular morphology exhibited the highest uptake capacity at 8.868 
mmol/g. This was followed by the Flower like morphology with 6.817 
mmol/g and the Cube morphology with 4.667 mmol/g. In comparison, 
the Block and Rod shaped morphologies demonstrated significantly 
lower capacities of 0.710 mmol/g and 0.928 mmol/g, respectively 
(Fig. 3b).
The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 3a) reveals the directional influence of 
key variables. For morphological categories including Granular and 
Cube, samples with a value of 1, represented in purple, clustered pre­
dominantly in the positive SHAP value region. Analysis of SHAP dis­
tributions (Fig. S6a) further shows that Granular, Flower-like, and Cube 
morphologies exhibit broader distributions with higher median values, 
suggesting significant contributions and potential feature interactions. 
In contrast, Flake and Sphere morphologies show more constrained in­
fluence, while Nest-like, Block, and Rod-shaped contributions are 
negligible. These results consistently underscore the pronounced 
advantage of Granular morphology. An interesting discrepancy was 
observed: although Flower-like morphology demonstrates high pre­
dicted uptake (Fig. S6b), its SHAP contribution remains modest, possibly 
due to its sample distribution or synergistic interactions with other 
features. Mechanistically, the superior performance of Granular 
morphology is attributed to its high surface area and abundant alkaline 
sites (Ding et al., 2015), whereas the hierarchical porous structure of 
Flower-like morphology facilitates synergistic effects between gas 
diffusion and surface reactions (Jin et al., 2020). Conversely, Block and 
Rod-shaped morphologies underperform due to structural limitations 
that restrict active site accessibility.
Process conditions were secondary in importance with a total 
contribution of 37.4%, primarily from time (23.3%) and temperature 
(14.1%) (Fig. 3b). The negligible contribution of pressure (0.1%) is 
attributed to its limited variability in the dataset, as most experiments 
were conducted under atmospheric conditions. Although time was the 
most influential individual variable, its global marginal effect was pre­
dominantly negative yet distinctly non-monotonic (Fig. 4a). A brief in­
crease in adsorption capacity within an intermediate time range was 
observed, followed by a gradual decline. This pattern confirms that the 
model successfully captured the inherent nonlinearity of adsorption 
kinetics. SHAP analysis revealed a consistently negative trend in the 
values associated with processing time, indicating an inverse statistical 
relationship between this variable and adsorption capacity within the 
experimental dataset of this study. This finding suggests that adsorbents 
demonstrating higher performance in this context typically exhibited 
faster adsorption kinetics. Temperature exhibited a clear positive cor­
relation with SHAP values (Fig. 3a), where higher (purple) and lower 
(green) temperatures corresponded to positive and negative SHAP 
values, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, the dependence followed a 
distinct unimodal pattern: the influence was negative at low tempera­
tures, turned positive to reach a peak at intermediate temperatures, and 
became negative again upon further heating. This pattern signifies a 
shift in the rate-controlling mechanism of MgO carbonation. At lower 
temperatures, physical adsorption dominates, while chemical adsorp­
tion prevails as temperature rises. A moderate temperature increase 
enhances reaction kinetics; however, excessively high temperatures ul­
timately inhibit the reaction due to the increasingly unfavorable Gibbs 
free energy of carbonation (Ji et al., 2020). The analysis further
