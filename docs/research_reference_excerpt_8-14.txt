elucidated the nuanced nature of variable influences. The identified 
dependence of adsorption capacity on temperature aligned with 
well-established physical mechanisms, thereby reinforcing the reli­
ability of the model. Conversely, the relationship with processing time 
predominantly reflected a strong intrinsic statistical correlation within 
the data. This correlation points to a link between superior adsorbent 
performance and rapid kinetics, rather than implying a direct causal 
physical influence of time duration itself.
The combined contribution of pore structure variables was 15.3%, 
distributed among pore volume (6.5%), pore size (6.2%), and specific 
surface area (2.5%) (Fig. 3b). Pore volume and pore size exhibited 
progressive transitions from low to high values, indicating their positive 
influence on adsorption performance. In contrast, the SHAP values for 
SBET and particle size clustered near zero, consistent with their low 
importance. The SHAP dependence for SBET (Fig. 4c) revealed a coun­
terintuitive pattern: a positive marginal effect in the low-value region 
that rapidly decreased, turned negative, and plateaued at high values. 
This can be attributed to the ineffective accessibility of surface area in 
materials with ultra-micropores, tortuous channels, or sintered pores, 
where the nominal SBET does not equate to the effectively accessible area 
(Heo and Park, 2017). This aligns with explainable ML studies in other 
adsorption systems, which have similarly captured the diminishing 
marginal effect of SBET (Teng and Shan, 2024; Xie et al., 2023). 
Conversely, Vpore showed a predominantly monotonic positive rela­
tionship with SHAP values (Fig. 4d), underscoring its critical role. Larger 
pore volumes provide more internal space and adsorption sites, facili­
tating CO2 access and accommodation (Heo and Park, 2017). Further­
more, dpore exhibited a non-monotonic dependence with an optimal 
mesoporous range (Fig. 4e), as evidenced by a significant positive peak 
at medium sizes, flanked by negative influences at both lower and higher 
values.
Fig. 4f indicates an overall negative influence of particle size on the 
predicted adsorption capacity, suggesting a general preference for 
smaller particles. This was attributed to the increased specific surface 
area and exposure of active sites resulting from particle size reduction, a 
mechanism conducive to enhanced adsorption (Hiremath et al., 2017). A 
more nuanced analysis revealed that the most negative SHAP values 
occurred within the small-to-medium size range, indicating the greatest 
marginal gains in capacity from size reduction in this interval. This 
negative influence gradually attenuated to near zero with increasing 
particle size. This attenuation suggests a shift in the dominant adsorp­
tion mechanism from surface reaction control to other rate-limiting 
steps, such as intraparticle diffusion. Beyond a certain size, particle 
variation ceases to be a primary performance constraint, which aligns 
with its diminished importance in the model.
PDP and ALE analyses were employed to evaluate dopant effects 
(Fig. 5). PDP quantified the average predicted adsorption capacity for 
each dopant, while ALE measured the local deviation from the average, 
with positive (enhancement) and negative (suppression) values. Using 
the undoped adsorbent (PDP = 2.884, ALE = −0.562) as a baseline, most 
dopants exhibited higher PDP values, confirming that doping generally 
enhanced CO2 adsorption and validated the material design strategy. 
However, ALE values varied considerably among dopants, indicating 
mechanistic diversity in their functional roles.
Surfactants (SDS, CTAB, PPV) demonstrated both high PDP values 
(4.16 - 4.23 mmol/g) and stable positive ALE values (0.59 - 0.60). These 
modifiers enhanced the average CO2 adsorption capacity by improving 
key adsorbent properties such as specific surface area, pore architecture, 
and alkaline site distribution, thereby inducing consistent localized 
enhancement effects (Firmansyah et al., 2025). Similarly, amine-based 
reagents (APTES, DETA, PEI) exhibited closely clustered high PDP 
values (4.18 - 4.23 mmol/g) and remarkably consistent ALE values 
(0.597 - 0.600). This strong agreement indicated that amine incorpo­
ration provided numerous homogeneous chemical adsorption sites for 
CO2 capture. The underlying mechanism involved amine coupling and 
structural penetration, which enlarged the pore size and volume of the 
MgO matrix, thereby exposing more functionalized adsorption sites 
(Alkadhem et al., 2020).

Analysis of transition metal doping revealed a non-monotonic rela­
tionship with a well-defined optimal molar ratio. Using Mn as an 
example, the 0.1 M ratio condition yielded the highest PDP (4.321 
mmol/g) and a strong positive ALE (0.739). However, increasing the 
ratio to 0.15 substantially reduced the ALE to 0.112 (PDP = 4.053

enhancement. This was attributed to the formation of highly dispersed 
MgO nanocrystallites with increased specific surface area and the in-situ 
carbon matrix functioning as an effective stabilizer that inhibited MgO 
crystallite agglomeration (Othman et al., 2021).
Alkali metal nitrate dopants exhibited complex performance effects. 
Their PDP values spanned a broad spectrum (3.05 - 4.408 mmol/g), 
while ALE values ranged from significantly negative (−0.535) to 
strongly positive (0.821), indicating high sensitivity to the specific 
cation type, ratio, and total concentration. High-performance composi­
tions, such as 0.1(0.3Li0.18Na0.52K)NO3 (PDP = 4.408, ALE = 0.625)

demonstrated exceptional capacity and robust local enhancement. In 
contrast, low-concentration doping formulations such as 0.005K (PDP =
2.938 mmol/g, ALE = −0.535) or imbalanced ratios performed near or 
below baseline, which was attributed to insufficient alkali content 
failing to effectively reduce the adsorption energy of key MgO crystal 
planes (Gui et al., 2024). The CO2 adsorption performance of the 
xFe-(0.3Li0.6Na0.1K)NO3 system showed a non-monotonic dependence 
on iron content. The PDP value peaked at 4.361 mmol/g for the 0.1 M 
ratio and generally declined as the content deviated from this optimum 
(0.02 - 0.8). The ALE values remained positive (0.562 - 0.800) for Fe 
contents of 0.1 - 0.8 but turned significantly negative (−0.489) at 0.02. 
This trend suggested an optimal Fe doping window (approx. 0.1 - 0.4 
mol%) that synergized with the nitrate matrix. Within this window, Fe 
species enhanced charge transfer and strengthened oxygen atom 
bonding on the MgO surface, promoting preferential binding with acti­
vated CO2 species (CO) (Gong et al., 2023). Conversely, insufficient Fe 
content provided inadequate catalytic effect, while excess Fe likely 
induced particle agglomeration or pore blockage, diminishing the pro­
motional effect.

3.2.2. Interpretability analysis of interaction effects
Analysis of the synergistic effects between temperature and time 
revealed that the PDP plot reached a maximum adsorption capacity of 
4.5 mmol/g under moderate temperatures (200 - 400 ◦C) combined with 
adsorption times of 100 - 250 min (Fig. 6a). This finding was corrobo­
rated by ALE analysis, which showed significant positive deviations 
within this parameter range (Fig. 7a), indicating that such conditions 
not only achieved superior absolute adsorption performance but also 
generated relative gains exceeding the baseline. The moderate temper­
ature range represented an optimal balance between reaction driving 
force and intrinsic activity in gas - solid reactions, while appropriate 
durations ensured sufficient CO2 diffusion and the attainment of 
adsorption equilibrium. Conversely, temperatures exceeding 450 ◦C 
likely increased the Gibbs free energy and weakened the reaction driving 
force, whereas temperatures below 150 ◦C primarily facilitated physical 
adsorption. Adsorption times shorter than 50 min prevented the system 
from reaching equilibrium, while prolonged durations beyond 250 min 
could induce structural fatigue in the adsorbent. These extreme condi­
tions collectively led to a notable decline in adsorption performance.
A significant structural dependency was observed in the interaction 
between pore volume and particle size. PDP analysis indicated that 
combining large pore volume (>0.6 cm3/g) with moderate particle size 
(2000 - 8000 nm) yielded a high adsorption capacity of 7.0 mmol/g 
(Fig. 6b), consistent with porous material adsorption theory. Sufficient 
pore volume provides ample space for CO2 accommodation, while 
moderate particle size balances diffusion path length against external 
surface area. ALE analysis further revealed a positive deviation for small 
particle sizes (<1000 nm) combined with moderate pore volume (0.4 - 
0.8 cm3/g) (Fig. 7b), suggesting enhanced adsorption kinetics from 
shortened diffusion paths. Notably, even with optimal particle size, 
insufficient pore volume (<0.4 cm3/g) hindered efficient adsorption, 
underscoring the dominant role of pore volume. Conversely, excessively 
large particles (>8000 nm) likely limited adsorption rates due to 
extended diffusion paths, highlighting the necessity of precise structural 
parameter matching.
Analysis of temperature and particle size interactions revealed a 
complex coupling between thermodynamic and structural parameters. 
PDP analysis identified an optimal range of medium temperatures (100 - 
300 ◦C) and medium particle sizes (2000 - 6000 nm), achieving an 
adsorption capacity of approximately 5.0 mmol/g (Fig. 6c). In contrast, 
ALE analysis showed a negative deviation within this same region 
(Fig. 7c). This discrepancy reflected performance evaluations based on 
different reference frameworks: while this parameter combination 
yielded high absolute adsorption, its relative performance gain was 
suboptimal. Further analysis indicated that combining high temperature 
(>600 ◦C) with small particle size (<1000 nm) exhibited a weakly 
positive ALE deviation. This was attributed to the synergy between the 
rapid thermal response of small particles and enhanced activation at 
elevated temperatures. Although the absolute adsorption capacity 
remained moderate, this configuration still provided improvement 
relative to the baseline.
The particle size-time interaction was weaker than other parameter 
pairs. Both PDP and ALE analyses identified medium particle size (1000 
- 3000 nm) with moderate duration (100 - 250 min) as optimal (Fig. 6d 
and 7d), achieving 3.0 mmol/g adsorption capacity with positive ALE 
deviation. This suggests medium particles balance diffusion resistance 
with surface area utilization, while sufficient duration enables equilib­
rium. Conversely, extended time (>300 min) with large particles 
(>5000 nm) showed strong negative deviation, attributed to slow 
diffusion kinetics and reduced efficiency from excessive contact.
The temperature-pore volume interaction showed the strongest 
synergy. Combining moderate temperature (200 - 400 ◦C) with moder­
ate pore volume (0.4 - 0.8 cm3/g) yielded 5.5 mmol/g capacity (Fig. 6e) 
with significant positive ALE deviation (Fig. 7e), highlighting the crucial 
thermodynamics-structure match. Optimal temperature activates basic 
sites while adequate pore volume provides sufficient sites without sur­
face density dilution. Performance declined under low temperature 
(<150 ◦C) with small pores (<0.3 cm3/g) due to insufficient sites and

The interaction of morphology with temperature and time revealed 
distinct adsorption characteristics (Fig. 8). Granular morphology 
exhibited a unique bimodal thermal response, maintaining high capacity 
(nearly 8 mmol/g at <100 ◦C; 7 mmol/g at 300 ◦C) due to abundant 
active sites and stable pores. In contrast, Cube morphology showed a 
unimodal distribution, achieving 7.5 mmol/g at low temperatures but 
declining to 4 mmol/g above 300 ◦C, indicating better suitability for 
low-temperature operation. Flower-like morphology maintained stable 
but limited capacity (about 3 mmol/g) across temperatures, demon­
strating good thermal tolerance. Other morphologies (Block, Flake) 
showed low, steady performance with minimal temperature sensitivity.
Temporally, Granular morphology demonstrated sustained growth 
to 8 mmol/g at 100 min with excellent stability, indicating favorable 
kinetics. Cube morphology displayed a distinct optimal window peaking 
at 150 min (6.5 mmol/g) but declined significantly beyond 250 min. 
Flower-like morphology showed prominent short-term performance 
(5.5 mmol/g at <150 min) but decreased to 3 mmol/g under prolonged 
operation, suggesting suitability for rapid adsorption applications. Other 
morphologies maintained consistently low capacities (<3 mmol/g) 
throughout the time range.

System-specific variations were observed: Fe-based nitrate doping 
required >200 ◦C for effective activation, while multi-metal composite 
doping maintained efficiency across 250-350 ◦C, indicating enhanced 
thermal stability from component synergy.

3.3. Limitations and future work
While the ETFM framework developed in this study demonstrated 
strong predictive performance and mechanistic interpretability, several 
limitations were identified, highlighting valuable directions for future 
research. The predictive accuracy and interpretability of the model are 
inherently contingent upon the material systems and process parameter 
ranges encompassed by the current training dataset. As a data-driven 
approach, its reliability for extrapolation to novel material composi­
tions or extreme operating conditions remains uncertain. Although 
ensemble modeling was employed to enhance robustness, the extrapo­
lative capability of the framework necessitates further validation using 
independent, external datasets under such challenging scenarios. 
Regarding mechanistic interpretation, existing methods face difficulties 
in quantitatively disentangling complex interaction effects that involve 
high-cardinality categorical variables, such as complex doping systems. 
This limitation constrains a more granular and holistic understanding of

To address these limitations, future work will prioritize several key 
objectives. The expansion and refinement of the dataset are essential to 
ensure more balanced coverage of critical variable combinations and to 
mitigate potential biases. More rigorous validation protocols should be 
implemented to thoroughly assess the real-world generalizability of the 
model. There is also a need to explore or develop novel analytical 
methods capable of quantifying complex interactions among high- 
cardinality categorical variables, which would enable deeper mecha­

incorporation of confidence interval estimation and out-of-domain 
detection features. These advancements are aimed at evolving the 
framework into a more robust and intelligent decision-support system 
for the accelerated development of CO2 adsorbents.
4. Conclusion
This study developed and validated a machine learning framework 
that integrated the entropy -TOPSIS methodology for the accurate pre­
diction of the CO2 adsorption capacity of MgO-based adsorbents. The 
results demonstrated that the ETFM model achieved exceptional pre­
dictive performance on the test set, with an R2 value of 0.9903, and MSE, 
RMSE, and MAE values of 0.361, 0.6008, and 0.2544, respectively. This 
performance signifies a substantial enhancement in both prediction 
accuracy and robustness. Interpretability analysis via SHAP identified 
material morphology as the most influential feature, contributing 43.8% 
to the model output, with granular morphology demonstrating superior 
performance. Process parameters, namely adsorption time and temper­
ature, as well as pore structure characteristics, including pore volume 
and size, were also determined to be key variables. In contrast, the 
specific surface area exhibited a non-monotonic relationship with the 
adsorption capacity, characterized by decreasing marginal effects. These 
findings underscore the critical importance of engineering an effective 
pore architecture over the mere maximization of the specific surface 
area. Furthermore, an in-depth analysis of the complex nonlinear and 
interaction effects among the variables was conducted using PDP and 
ALE. This analysis identified critical parameter ranges conducive to 
performance optimization. For instance, the synergistic combination of 
a moderate temperature range of 200 - 400 ◦C with an adsorption 
duration of 100 - 250 min was identified as optimal for maximizing the 
adsorption capacity. Moreover, this work systematically evaluated the 
efficacy and mechanisms of various doping and modification strategies. 
The evaluation established that metal oxides, such as CeO2 and Al2O3, 
and alkali metal nitrates with specific molar ratios constitute optimal 
choices for enhancing performance. Conversely, transition metal doping 
is characterized by a narrow optimal concentration window.
